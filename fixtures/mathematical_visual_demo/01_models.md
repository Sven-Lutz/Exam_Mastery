## Learning objectives and prerequisites

## Discrete retention and a complete derivation
A nonnegative starting mass M (mg) and fixed retention r in [0,1] give M_1=rM and M_2=rM_1=r^2 M after two full-hour steps. Thus second-hour loss L_1=M_1-M_2=Mr-Mr^2=Mr(1-r). Here M and L_1 are masses in mg, r is dimensionless. The model only specifies integer-hour states; do not add fractional-hour interpolation. At M=10 and r=0.8: M_1=8 mg, M_2=6.4 mg, L_1=1.6 mg. At M=20, same r: L_1=3.2 mg. At r=0 or 1, L_1=0; a quotient relative to a zero starting mass is undefined. For a meaningful numerical lab vary M from 5 to 20 mg at fixed r=0.8; ask a fixed prediction 10 -> 20 before revealing results. Plot second-hour loss versus M, with units mg on both axes, using this same expression. This is a family of separate initial conditions, not continuous time. Synthetic transfer: M=5,r=0.6 gives M_1=3,M_2=1.8, losses 2 and 1.2 mg; both fractional losses 40%.

## Matrix model and least squares
Let A=[[1,0],[1,1],[1,2]] be a real 3-by-2 design matrix, x=(a,b)^T a real 2-vector, and y=(1,2,2)^T a synthetic real 3-vector. All three are dimensionless in this example. Ax has components a, a+b, a+2b. Define J(x)=1/2 ||Ax-y||_2^2. The unconstrained problem is min over x in R^2 of J(x). Component differentiation gives gradient A^T(Ax-y). At a minimum the normal equations are A^T A x=A^T y. Since columns of A are independent, A^T A=[[3,3],[3,5]] is positive definite and the stationary point is the unique minimizer. A^T y=(5,6)^T, so a=7/6, b=1/2. The fitted vector is (7/6,5/3,13/6)^T. Residuals fitted minus y are (1/6,-1/3,1/6)^T, hence J=1/12. The normal-equation formula alone is not a numerically preferred algorithm for ill-conditioned large systems; no general numerical-stability claim is made. ## Finite sums, integration and a proof boundary
For integer n>=1, sum_{k=1}^n k=n(n+1)/2. Base n=1 gives 1. If the statement holds at n, adding n+1 gives n(n+1)/2+(n+1)=(n+1)(n+2)/2. This is an induction proof, not merely a few numerical examples. Separately, integral from 0 to 1 of t^2 dt=1/3 by the antiderivative t^3/3. The derivative limit ((t+h)^2-t^2)/h tends to 2t for real t as nonzero h approaches zero; expand and cancel before taking the limit. Define every index and limiting variable.
