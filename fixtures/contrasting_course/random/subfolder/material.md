# Synthetic development material: logic and biological evidence
This is authored synthetic test material, not an unseen lecture or an official course.

## Logic learning objectives
Distinguish implication from its converse using counterexamples. For propositions P and Q, P implies Q is false exactly when P is true and Q false. Its converse Q implies P does not follow. Example: all squares are rectangles; a rectangle with unequal adjacent sides is not a square. A counterexample refutes a universal assertion, but a finite set of confirming examples does not prove it.

## Biology learning objectives
Interpret controlled membrane transport observations and distinguish observation from inference. In an idealized classroom model a selectively permeable membrane passes water but no solute. Water shows net movement from lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration when pressure and temperature are initially equal. This simplified model omits hydrostatic pressure feedback and membrane-specific permeability. It is not a clinical model. With A containing 0.1 mol/L and B 0.3 mol/L, the initial net water movement is A to B. This is a qualitative prediction; a rate cannot be calculated from concentrations alone.

## Exercises and solutions
1. Does rectangle imply square? Solution: no; sides 2 and 3 give a counterexample. Do not confuse a converse with a contrapositive.
2. Reverse the initial concentrations in the model. Solution: the initial net direction reverses; no numerical rate is implied.
3. Transfer: a proposed universal biological rule is supported by ten observations. Does that logically prove it? Solution: no; finite confirmations alone do not establish a universal proposition. A single valid counterexample can refute a universal assertion. Measurement uncertainty and the rule's stated domain must be checked.
